# Slow Oven Red Sauce — Three-Stream Build

**Yield ~1.3–1.4 L · Active ~40 min · Total ~3 h 15 · One 24–26 cm braiser + Miele convection**

Built on Kenji's *Best Slow-Cooked Italian-American Tomato Sauce*, restructured around
two things you already do: **whole-clove garlic confit** and **fresh tomatoes roasted
for caramelization**. Scaled to **1 kg fresh + 2 × 800 g tins**.

The base recipe runs one stream. This runs **three that concentrate under different
physics**, then merges them.

| Stream | Vessel | Physics | Contributes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Garlic confit** | Small covered dish, submerged in oil | Slow browning at low water activity | Sweet, non-pungent garlic + infused fat |
| **Roasted fresh** | One sheet tray, cut side up | High surface-to-volume, fast evaporation, direct caramelization | Sugar browning, jammy fruit, tray fond |
| **Oven braise** | One wide braiser, uncovered | Slow concentration + Maillard on the vessel wall | Depth, body, the red-sauce floor |

## What is mine and what is Kenji's

I got this wrong twice in earlier drafts, so it is now explicit.

**The original** (verified against two independent verbatim transcriptions, since
seriouseats.com can't be fetched directly): olive oil and butter, minced garlic sweated
in the pot, dried oregano and pepper flakes, tinned whole tomatoes with some of that
tomato stirred back raw at the end, one halved onion and carrot chunks (discarded at
the end), basil stems, fish sauce **optional and only at the end**, minced parsley and
basil to finish. Lid slightly ajar, a long cook, stirred along the way, reduced by
about half. The cup and tablespoon measures from those transcriptions are filed under
Confidence. They are not the doses in this pot.

**Not in the original, whatever I said earlier: no anchovy, and no Parmigiano rind.**
I attributed both to it. Both were mine.

**My departures, and why:**

| Change | Reason |
|---|---|
| Garlic confit whole, added late | Your method. Sweeter than minced-and-sweated, and late addition keeps the volatiles |
| Roasted fresh as a separate stream | Your method. Tray geometry is the only way fresh tomato browns |
| Parmigiano rind added | My addition. Glutamate + a little body |
| **Lid off, not ajar** | Deliberate intensification: more wall fond, faster reduction. **Also more scorch risk.** Ajar is the safer original |
| **135 °C fan** | The dial to set. Fan runs about 20 °C hotter in effect than the same number on a conventional oven, a little above the original, and one shallow pot does in ~1.5 h what the original does in 5–6 |
| Reserved raw tomato, about a fifth | The original holds back about the same fraction and stirs it in raw at the end. Kept |

## Read this before you shop

**Indigo Rose turns purple before it is ripe inside.** The anthocyanin develops on a
schedule unrelated to ripeness, and underripe fruit is consistently reported as bland
and watery. No amount of roasting rescues it. This is the biggest failure risk in the
recipe and it happens at the market.

- **Shoulder** — shiny blue-black is *not ready*. You want it dulled toward
  purple-brown.
- **Blossom end** — the real tell. Ripens bottom-up. You want red-to-orange spreading
  up from the base over at least half the fruit.
- **Feel** — genuine give. Hard means green inside no matter how black the top is.

**The skins.** An earlier draft of this told you the skins were thin and to leave them
in. **That was wrong** — I had reversed a correct call. Indigo Rose is reported as
notably **thick-skinned**, and at least one published taste panel described the skin as
*thick and tannic, lending bitterness*. Oregon State, who bred it, also note the
anthocyanin sits in skin **and outer flesh** and **leaches out during cooking**.

**So: food-mill the roasted fresh, medium plate.** The reasoning is asymmetric risk, not
certainty — the evidence on skin bitterness is one taste panel, but milling costs you
only some caramelized skin, while being wrong the other way costs you 1.4 L of faintly
bitter sauce. **You keep the color either way**, because the pigment has already leached
into the flesh by then.

**Check your tin sodium.** "Premium whole peeled" spans a 5× range and it changes what
you do at the finish. See the ledger.

## Ingredients

### Garlic confit
- Garlic, whole peeled cloves — **65 g** (~1½ heads)
- Olive oil, enough to submerge — **200 ml** *(you'll use 50 ml; keep the rest)*

### Roasted fresh — one tray
- Indigo Rose, **ripe**, halved — **1 kg**
- Olive oil — **25 ml**
- Fine salt — **2 g**

### Oven braise
- Whole peeled tomatoes — **1.6 kg** (2 × 800 g tins). **1.3 kg** into the braise, **300 g** of that same tomato held raw until the merge
- Olive oil — **30 ml**
- Unsalted butter — **20 g** (+ **20 g** at the finish)
- Onion, halved through the root — **1 large / ~250 g**
- Carrot, peeled, whole — **1 large / ~100 g**
- Parmigiano-Reggiano rind — **40 g** *(my addition)*
- Dried oregano — **2.5 g**
- Chili flakes — **1.5 g**
- Basil — **1 bunch**. Stems into the braise, leaves from that bunch at the end
- Fine salt — **1.5 g up front only**

### Merge and finish
- Fish sauce — **12 ml**. **8 ml** at the merge, the last **4 ml** at the finish, none in the braise
- Salt — **0–5 g**, 1 g at a time, only when the salt step names it
- Sugar — **0–6 g**, only if it is still sharp after that salt

## Salt ledger

Computed, not measured. Finished mass ~1.42 kg; target **0.85 %** salt equivalent
(a judgment call for a pasta sauce, not a rule). Sodium → salt = **×2.542**.

Fixed contributions: fish sauce **3.0 g** (Red Boat 40°N at 1,490 mg Na/Tbsp, USDA
branded label) · parm rind **0.4 g** (Parmigiano-Reggiano DOP at 650 mg Na/100 g,
Consorzio figure, partial leach) · tray salt **2.0 g** · braise salt **1.5 g**
= **6.9 g before the tomatoes.**

| Your tin | Na/100 g | From tin | Total | % | **Likely stop** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| No-salt-added (Muir Glen etc.) | ~0 | 0 g | 6.9 g | 0.49 % | **~5 g** |
| Cento San Marzano | ~33 | 1.3 g | 8.3 g | 0.58 % | **~4 g** |
| Mutti Pelati / Casa Marrazzo DOP | ~40 | 1.6 g | 8.6 g | 0.60 % | **~3.5 g** |
| Bianco DiNapoli | ~149 | 6.1 g | 13.0 g | 0.91 % | **0 g** |

Most premium DOP tins sit near the bottom of this range — **Bianco DiNapoli is the
outlier at roughly 4× Mutti or Cento.** The last column is where that tin usually
lands. Add **1 g** at a time and stop on the taste, even if you are short of it. If
you don't know the tin, taste your way. You will probably land near 4 g. Note this is
*Parmigiano-Reggiano* rind; generic grating "parmesan" runs ~2.5× the sodium.

## Method

### 1. Garlic confit — 160 °C fan, covered, 35–55 min

**65 g** peeled cloves, single layer in a small dish, **fully submerged under 200 ml
olive oil, covered tightly with foil.**

Submerged, a clove has almost no exposed water surface and colors evenly. Covered
matters because **160 °C is hotter than a confit really wants** — I'm running the garlic
at the tomatoes' temperature so they can share the oven, and the foil is what makes that
safe. Uncovered at this temperature the exposed tops go dark before the centers sweeten.

**Check at 35 min, then every 8.** Target: **deep gold, collapsing under light spoon
pressure, sweet with no raw bite.** Lift out, reserve **50 ml** of the oil, strain and
keep the rest (fridge, ~2 weeks).

### 2. Roast the fresh — 160 °C fan ~75 min, then 210 °C 12–18 min

Halve **1 kg** ripe Indigo Rose through the equator. Toss with **25 ml** oil and **2 g**
salt, lay **cut side up, single layer, not touching.**

**1 kg fits one tray comfortably** — ~800–1,000 cm² of fruit against ~1,580 cm² of Miele
tray. (This is why the batch size helps: the 2.5 kg version needed three trays.)

**Weigh the tray before it goes in.** Published figures for roasting loss are all over
the place and I'm not going to assert one. **Pull at roughly half the starting tomato
weight** — that's a measurement, not a guess.

The hot bump goes last on purpose: while free water sits on the cut face, that surface is
pinned near 100 °C and can't brown. Only after 75 minutes of drying does 210 °C do
anything.

- **Slumped, edges browned, tray fond amber-to-brown and sticky** → done.
- **Glossy, plump, tray liquid thin** → not done. 15 more min.
- **Cut faces black, fond black and smoking** → overshot. Pull, and don't scrape black
  fond into the pot.

**Deglaze the tray** with ~40 ml water, scraping hard. **Food-mill the tomatoes, medium
plate**, and stir the deglaze into the pulp.

### 3. Build the braiser — stovetop, from 1:00

**One 24–26 cm braiser.** At this batch size that's a ~2.5–3 cm layer, which is the right
depth: shallow enough for real wall fond, deep enough that it won't run away from you.
A 30 cm pan would put you under 2 cm and reduce faster than you can watch.

Warm **30 ml** oil and **20 g** butter over medium. Add the **oregano** and **chili
flakes**, bloom **30 s** — fragrant, not colored.

Hand-crush **1.3 kg** of tinned tomato in. Add the **halved onion**, **whole carrot**,
**parm rind**, **basil stems**, and **1.5 g salt only.** No fish sauce yet.

*Onion and carrot go in whole and come out whole* — sweetness without becoming texture.
Dice them and you get a heavier soffritto sauce that blunts the tomato.

Bring to a **bare simmer, then kill the heat and cover** until the oven frees up. Don't
hold a tomato braise over direct heat on enamel unattended.

### 4. The oven braise — 135 °C fan, uncovered, ~1 h 15 to 2 h

**Endpoint is volume, not time: reduced by about half, darkened to deep red.** That's the
original's endpoint and it's the right one. **First check at 60 min, then every 20.** I
can't give you a reliable clock figure at this scale and vessel — anyone who does is
guessing.

**Every 20–30 min, scrape the crust on the wall above the sauce** and stir it in.
That crust is tomato cooked onto the enamel. **It is the recipe** — Maillard on tomato
solids, and the only step producing flavors tomatoes don't already contain.

- **Deep red-brown, releases when scraped, smells sweet and roasted** → right.
- **Black, brittle, smells acrid or like burnt coffee** → scorched. Drop to 120 °C, stir
  every 15. Don't scrape the black in; sugar won't fix it.
- **No crust on the wall after 45 min** → bump to 150 °C.

*If this makes you nervous, set the lid slightly ajar as the original does.* You'll get
less fond and a longer cook, and you'll be much harder to burn.

### 5. Merge

Discard the **onion, carrot, basil stems**. Squeeze the **parm rind** against the pot
side and discard.

Stir in, all of it:

1. The **milled roasted fresh** and its deglaze.
2. The **300 g** reserved tinned tomato, hand-crushed. That is the tomato you held back, not another tin.
3. The **confit garlic** (crush two-thirds against the wall, leave the rest whole).
4. **50 ml** of the confit oil. The rest of the 200 ml stays in the jar.
5. **8 ml** of the fish sauce. The last **4 ml** waits for the finish.

*All five do the same job.* Long oven time builds depth and destroys brightness in the
same motion. These go last because their volatiles would have gone hours ago — which
is also why the original adds its fish sauce at the end.

Back in, **135 °C, 20–30 min**, uncovered.

### 6. Reduction gate

**Drag a spoon across the bottom of the pot.** Look at the path it leaves, in the pot.
A tasting spoon cools, thickens, and lies to you.

- **The path fills in after about 2 seconds** → right for pasta.
- **The path disappears at once, or thin orange liquid sits at the edge** → 15 more min.
  Don't season yet.
- **The path stays open and the sauce sits in a mound** → over-reduced for pasta.
  Loosen with 50–100 ml water.

### 7. Texture — off heat

**Immersion blender, 4–5 short pulses, about half homogenized.** Smooth enough to cling
and emulsify, still carrying visible pieces.

**Keep the head fully submerged and the speed moderate.** Whipping air into tomato sauce
lightens it toward orange — that's the documented effect, and it's about aeration, not
about which blender. A surfacing immersion head does it just as readily as a jar blender.

### 8. Finish — off heat

1. **20 g cold butter**, cubed, stirred in. Texture and acid as much as richness:
   emulsified fat rounds perceived sharpness and gives the gloss that carries onto pasta.
2. **The last 4 ml fish sauce.** Taste before and after. The salt ledger assumes this
   4 ml is already in the pot.
3. **Salt**, 1 g at a time. Taste between them. Stop when it tastes savory, even if you
   are short of the likely stop.
   - Mutti, Cento, most DOP → you will probably stop near **4 g**.
   - No-salt-added → nearer **5 g**.
   - Bianco DiNapoli → taste before any salt. You may be done at **none**.
4. **Sugar**, only if it is still sharp after that salt. 2 g steps, ceiling ~6 g.
   An under-salted sauce reads sour before it reads bland, so the salt is settled
   before any sugar goes in. **Skip baking soda** — America's Test Kitchen
   blind-tasted it against sugar and found it flat and one-dimensional even at ⅛ tsp.
   (It probably dulls color too, via pH-driven browning, but I couldn't find a real
   test for that — treat it as mechanism, not finding.)
5. **Basil**, the leaves from the bunch, torn in off heat at the last moment.

**If your Indigo Rose came in underripe**, expect the full sugar allowance and less
perfume. That's the fruit, not the method.

### 9. Serve and store

**Pasta:** cook it *2 min short*, into a pan with sauce and *50–70 ml pasta water*, toss
hard over medium 60–90 s until it tightens onto the noodles. **~1.35 L sauces roughly
1.3–1.6 kg of dry pasta**, or 6–8 servings.

**Storage:** fridge 5 days, freezer 6 months. Kenji freezes this exact sauce, butter and
all, and reheats gently with a splash of water. It **may separate on thaw** — whisk it
back over low heat with 50 ml water. Earlier drafts of this told you a frozen butter
emulsion "breaks and goes grainy" and to freeze un-buttered; that oversold it. Tomato
pectin stabilizes a low-fat sauce like this one. Freeze it however you like.

## Red team

- **Flavorless.** Underripe Indigo Rose. Happens at the market, not the stove.
- **Bitter.** Scorched fond stirred in (unrecoverable — hence the 20-minute checks),
  over-browned garlic (recoverable if you sort the dark cloves out), or skins left in.
- **Scorched, generally.** Most likely at this small scale: a shallow layer in a wide
  pan at 135 °C fan can go from reducing to catching quickly. Use 24–26 cm, not 30, and
  set the lid ajar if you'd rather trade fond for margin.
- **Thin on the plate.** Under-reduction. The path the spoon leaves still disappears at once.
- **Flat despite the long cook.** You shorted the merge. Long cooking destroys brightness
  as it builds depth; step 5 is the counterweight.
- **Over-salted.** Salt only after reduction stops.
- **Orange, not red.** Aeration during blending.

## Levers

- **Soffritto version** — fine-dice half an onion and half a carrot, sweat first, keep
  the whole ones too. Heavier, sweeter, more Sunday gravy. Better under meatballs.
- **Meat version** — brown sausage, braise from step 3, pull at step 5. Add ~80 ml water.
- **Higher fresh ratio** — push to 1.5 kg fresh, drop to 1 tin plus the reserve. More
  perfume, less spine. Don't go below one full tin's worth into the braise.
- **Calabrian chili paste**, 6 g at the merge, for heat with fruit behind it.
- **Smoke** — char half the fresh on the plancha instead of the tray.

## Wine

High acid and savory fruit over tannin: Chianti Classico, Aglianico del Vulture, Barbera
d'Alba, or a Frappato if you want it lighter. The butter and rind give enough mid-palate
weight that a big tannic red would clamp down on the tomato acid. Say the word and I'll
pull bottles and windows from the cellar.
