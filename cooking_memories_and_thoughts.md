# Cooking — Memories & Thoughts

*A running commonplace book for this kitchen. Not recipes — those live in their own folders.
This is the reasoning behind them: what we've cooked, what each dish taught, and the principles
that have accreted from doing the work. Kept because a project that keeps its methodology gets
better; one that only keeps its outputs repeats its mistakes.*

---

## The dishes, and what they left behind

**Morel pappardelle, cream & Parmigiano.** A triple-reduction French-Italian build — wine, stock,
cream in sequence — around fresh morels in their short season. It cooked well the first time, then
got red-teamed into something better: acid was the missing structural element (a squeeze of lemon
and zest at the pass), a 20 g cold-butter mount went in before the cheese for gloss, the garlic
came out because it crowded the one flavor we were paying a premium for, and the sear instruction
was rewritten to insist on a ripping-hot pan so the morels caramelize instead of steaming. The
lesson that generalized: **richness needs a counterweight, and the counterweight is almost always
acid.** It shows up again and again below.

**Eggplant parmigiana, Neapolitan-style.** Roasted, not breaded — the whole dish organized around
moisture control as the central problem. Purge, roast, a proper sugo, bake, and a real rest before
cutting so it sets into slices instead of sliding into slop. *Open thread: the markdown made it to
iCloud but the cook-live HTML transfer was interrupted and never finished. Worth completing.*

**Beef bourguignon.** The most instructive thing we've done, because it failed. Full post-mortem
below — it earned its own section.

**North African harissa-tomato braised chicken.** A base-swap off Brad Leone's coconut braise,
pushed toward a tomato-harissa register. Three corrections carried the day: a salt-timing trap from
stacking three salty components, tomato paste scorching at high heat unless properly caramelized
first, and a triple-acid stack with no coconut fat left to buffer it. Cooked in the Le Creuset
tagine, lid off, single layer, liquid kept below the skin line. **Salt and acid both stack across
ingredients — you have to audit the whole build, not each addition in isolation.**

**24-hour flank steak marinade.** Soy and fish sauce, deliberately low on acid — rice vinegar held
to 15 ml, citrus kept off the meat entirely — because acid over a long soak breaks the surface
down to mush. The useful catch: fish sauce carries roughly double the sodium per ml of soy, so the
total salt load was aggressive for a thin cut over 24 h. Dropped soy 80 → 60 ml, added 20 ml water,
kept the fish sauce, moved the flaky salt to the board as a finish. **Extended marinades are a salt
problem before they're a flavor problem.**

**Miso-braised chicken legs.** Three misos assigned three non-overlapping jobs — barley awase as
the braising backbone, jūwari kōji for finishing umami and body, white sweet for balance and the
optional lacquer — with the bright finishing misos added off the boil to keep their aromatics
intact. Cooked live: the dashi became a Korean seafood instant stock (soy cut against its built-in
salt), a fond-vs-scorch gate caught a questionable pot bottom before it could poison the sauce, and
the salt gate drove the finishing dose instead of the gram count. Then a vessel odyssey — enamel,
to a carbon-steel paella pan, and back to two enameled braisers — which crystallized a rule about
geometry (below). This is also where the **cook-it-live skill** got packaged into something
reusable.

## The bourguignon post-mortem — kept deliberately

We took a tested NYT recipe and made five sophisticated changes at once: gelatinous stock instead
of flour, wine pre-reduced before it entered the pot, sauce separated and reduced off the meat, a
lower braise temperature, added shank. None tested individually. The result tasted like thick
generic stock with no wine, the meat overshot to pulled-pork, and the sauce never set.

What actually went wrong:

- **A sealed pot with a cartouche cannot hold liquid below boiling.** Sealing kills the evaporative
  cooling that keeps a braise under 100 °C. The spec of 150 °C + sealed + cartouche targeting 88 °C
  was physically impossible. The honest version: 135 °C, no cartouche, probe at 20 min and nudge
  the oven ±15 °C to hold 93–96 °C.
- **Pre-reducing the wine front-loaded the exact loss it claimed to prevent.** Reduction destroys
  wine's top notes by the same mechanism braising does. In a braise where the wine *is* the flavor,
  reducing it first removes the point. (It's a fine technique for pan sauces, where wine is a
  component and stock is the base — not here.)
- **Removing the flour removed the safety margin.** The gelatin architecture only pays off if the
  stock sets to a jelly; with ordinary stock there was no fallback, and gelatin added to a sauce
  already below ~60 °C wouldn't dissolve. Beurre manié is what finally rescued it.

What it taught, beyond the specifics:

- **A self-audit that skips the load-bearing assumptions is worse than none** — it buys confidence
  it hasn't earned. The red-team covered salt and acid; it never touched the two decisions
  (temperature, wine) that actually determined the outcome.
- **Technique-stacking is not improvement.** Five clever changes at once is five new failure modes.
- **A tested recipe carries information that reasoning can't reconstruct.** The bar for departing
  from one should be high, and departures should be small, few, and individually justified.
- **Where a number can't be stated with confidence, give a target and a control loop.** "Hold
  93–96 °C, probe and adjust" survives a real kitchen. "150 °C" does not.

The working version reverted to the NYT base with four changes only: salt at 1%, 30 g tomato paste
properly caramelized, a third of the liquid as stock, garnishes cooked in separate pans.

## Principles that keep earning their place

- **Acid is structure, not garnish.** Nearly every rich dish here improved when acid went in at the
  finish — morel cream, the braises. Its job is to keep richness from reading heavy.
- **Salt and acid stack across the whole build.** Audit the sum, not the parts — the harissa
  chicken and the flank marinade both turned on this.
- **Late additions preserve what long cooking destroys.** Miso off the boil, herbs and acid at the
  pass, cheese last. Volatile aromatics don't survive an hour at temperature.
- **Probe, not clock.** Doneness is a temperature and a texture, not a duration. Dark meat to
  82–85 °C for collagen conversion, not the 74 °C minimum. Timers are floors.
- **Geometry decides the braise.** Liquid level is dosed to the vessel, not the ingredient list —
  it comes to mid-thigh with the skin proud, whatever volume that takes. Wide-and-shallow reduces
  fast and needs more liquid; deep-and-sealed needs less. Scaling up is never a clean multiplier.
- **Match the pan to the chemistry.** Enamel for anything salty or acidic — bare cast iron and
  carbon steel react over a long wet braise and throw metallic notes (the miso braise talked itself
  out of a beautiful carbon-steel paella pan for exactly this reason).
- **Assign non-overlapping roles.** Three misos, three jobs. Don't blend components that should each
  be doing distinct work.
- **Calibrate confidence to derivation.** A number that was computed and a number that was asserted
  should not be presented the same way. Flag pattern-matching as pattern-matching.

## How this kitchen works

Recipes get developed at a professional level of technique and often *in parallel with the cook* —
Claude as a stove-side guide flagging the judgment gates before they arrive, not a static card.
Every instruction opens with an action verb and carries a quantity or a time; reasoning lives in
the notes, not the action line. Before a recipe is locked it gets a structured red-team pass — acid
balance, fat structure, salt stacking, technique gaps, quantity calibration. Recommendations are
directional: a clear pick with reasoning, not a menu of hedged options. And when a number gets
challenged, the move is to audit it honestly rather than defend it — several of the best
corrections here came from exactly that.

## The kit & the cellar

High-end gas, a Miele convection oven, a microwave. Cast iron and carbon steel, enameled braisers
and larger pots, a Le Creuset tagine (cast-iron base, ceramic lid to 260 °C), a Smithey 17" carbon
steel paella pan, a wok. Metric is the working language throughout; no conversions.

Three Japanese misos worth keeping straight (Marumasa Jōzō / AKOMEYA): a **white sweet** (shiro),
sweetest and palest; a **jūwari kōji** (甘口つぶ), high-kōji rice miso, umami-forward; and a barley
**awase** (合わせ, 減塩), nuttier and earthier, low-salt.

The wine collection lives in CellarTracker. A full export sits in this folder as
`cellartracker-export-2026-09-16.csv`, 159 unique wines and 990 bottles, pulled 2026-09-16.
Reference it for any pairing question. Strong in Hiyu and Tzum (Hood River natural), Tissot (Jura), Henschke (Eden
Valley), Emidio Pepe, Paolo Bea, and select Bordeaux. The uploaded export may lag the actual cellar,
so corrections from the cook are authoritative. Standing pairing note: umami-forward marinades
converge with savory-fruited Syrah, not high-tannin Bordeaux.

## Cook-it-live, filed

The skill had been a pattern living inside the HTML files and nowhere else, so each new dish re-derived it. The slow-oven red sauce quietly dropped the phase rail, the vessel chip, and the quantity chips the miso braise had earned. It is now `cook-it-live/template.html`. The rules for deriving a dish from it, and the changelog, are `cook-it-live/SKILL.md`. Standing output rules for this kitchen are `AGENTS.md`.

The engine change that earned the filing: the timers counted down with `setInterval`, which a phone throttles or freezes when the screen locks. A braise timer that only advances while the tab is awake is not a timer. They now store an end timestamp and read the wall clock, restore that end time if the tab is killed and reopened, hold a screen wake lock while one is running, and raise a notification when it hits zero. Gates grew a third branch, `wait`, because "not yet" and "ruined" were sharing a color. Long oven steps adjust by minutes. A step can name what is still running on the other burner.

Every recipe from here is a markdown file and a cook-it-live HTML copied from that template. An engine change goes back into the template and the changelog. A reskin does not count.
